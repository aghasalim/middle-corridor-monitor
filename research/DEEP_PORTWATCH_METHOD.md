### What this changes for the paper

- The statement in section 3.1 that the port polygons are not published stands, and is now checked against the whole public service list, not one layer.
- The paper can say more about how polygons are drawn: by hand, round berths and the approach channel, from vessel tracks on satellite imagery, and since 2026 checked against port authorities' berth lists and sometimes widened to cover a group of ports. That is a documented reason why one PortWatch port can cover several terminals.
- Whether Alat is inside "Baku Port" is still not documented. Two facts bear on it, and both are inference, not proof. First, the port point is about 42 km from Alat, far more than a berth-and-channel polygon normally spans. Second, the Port of Baku says all its freight has been handled at Alat since 2018, yet PortWatch records about 500 Ro-Ro calls and about 680 general cargo calls a year at "Baku Port" in 2022. If Alat were outside, those calls would have to be at other quays round Baku (shipyards, oil terminals, lay-up berths). The second fact makes inclusion more likely than not, but it does not settle it.
- Section 3.1 says PortWatch "defines a port call as a ship entering a port boundary". PortWatch's own FAQ now says arrival at berth, and WP 26/099 says presence inside the polygon for a required time. The paper should quote both and say the exact rule is not published.
- The cheapest way to close the question is now clearer: WP 21/225 says boundaries are available from the authors on request. Only the user should send that request.

### What this changes for the paper

- Section 3.1 can be made firmer. The published precursor of PortWatch does not merely "keep trade-carrying vessels"; its type table explicitly removes the subtype `Passenger Ro Ro Cargo Ship` together with passenger and cruise ships, and keeps `Ro Ro Cargo Ship` and `Vehicles Carrier`. The IMF's 2026 paper confirms that passenger vessels are not covered by PortWatch.
- Section 6 says eight of the 14 identified ferries are listed as passenger/Ro-Ro ships and "may be outside PortWatch (unverified)". This can be upgraded to: excluded under the published 2021 type table; PortWatch's current table is not published, so exclusion today is likely but not confirmed.
- The paper should add two ways a ferry could still be counted, or counted under a different type over time: (i) the category depends on the subtype held in the FleetMon or IHS register, not on what the ship does, and (ii) where the subtype is missing it is predicted from dimensions and deadweight. Either could put a rail ferry into "Ro-Ro", "general cargo" or nothing. This fits the paper's own finding that PortWatch "Ro-Ro" at Aktau is not what the port counts as ferries.
- The Ro-Ro category was introduced in 2024 and adjusted a little in 2025, both applied to the whole history. A reclassification of this kind changes levels for all years at once; it does not by itself create a step in late 2021. The paper should say so, so that readers do not take the 2024 and 2025 notes as the explanation of the step.
- The register question in `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` #3 is answered as far as documents go: FleetMon and IHS Markit, with a model filling gaps. The exact field and code list remain unpublished.

### What this changes for the paper

- **Section 5.2 must be corrected.** It says "No PortWatch or IMF document that we read mentions a break in 2021 or 2022." PortWatch's FAQ now states that expanded receiver coverage in November 2021 produced a lasting rise in recorded port calls, and names two places. The IMF therefore accepts that coverage-driven level shifts exist in its series, in the very months where the paper's fitted breaks fall (November 2021 to January 2022). The FAQ item was last edited on 21 October 2026; the date the sentence was added is not known.
- **But the documented break does not look like the Caspian step, and the paper should say that too.** At the named port the break is a single day (9 November 2021) and lifts every ship type about fourfold. At Baku, Aktau and Turkmenbashi total calls do not jump in November 2021, general cargo is flat, and Ro-Ro starts to rise in July 2021 with a second lift in December 2021. So the FAQ proves that this kind of artefact exists; it does not prove that it explains the Caspian Ro-Ro step.
- The paper's wording "a change in the AIS data feed for the Caspian ... is the leading explanation" should be narrowed. What the evidence now supports is: (a) receiver-coverage changes in late 2021 are documented for PortWatch, elsewhere; (b) the Caspian step is confined to one ship type and starts earlier, which points at least as much to how particular vessels are identified or classified as to coverage; (c) neither is confirmed. One mechanism would reconcile the two and is worth stating as a hypothesis only: ferries turn round in hours, so sparse reception can miss their calls or leave them under the five-hour rule, while tankers and cargo ships that stay for days are still caught. Better reception would then raise Ro-Ro counts and leave the other types unchanged. No document says this.
- `LITERATURE.md` section 4 reads the phrase "two different satellite constellations" as a sign that exactEarth's constellation was added to a Spire feed after 2021. That reading is wrong. The identical sentence, with ExactEarth as the provider, is in the IMF paper of August 2021. ExactEarth was the platform's provider from the start; the provider name later changed to Spire and then Kpler through takeovers. No document dates any change in the content of the feed. `OPEN_QUESTIONS.md` #7 should be reworded: the question is not when exactEarth entered the feed, but whether Spire's own satellites or terrestrial data were added to it after the takeover, and when.
- Section 3.1 says the AIS data "come from Spire through the UN Global Platform". PortWatch's FAQ now names Kpler, and asks for "Kpler" in the citation line; the dataset description still says Spire. The paper should give the provider as exactEarth, later Spire, now Kpler, and cite the FAQ.
- The early-2021 trough (28 Ro-Ro calls at the three ports in January to June 2021, none at Aktau, and none at Poti from December 2020 to March 2021) remains unexplained by any document. The paper already reports it; it should keep it separate from the November 2021 coverage note.
- The recommendation at the end of section 5.2 stands: Ro-Ro calls before 2022 should not be compared with later ones. The reason can now cite PortWatch's own admission of coverage breaks instead of only the Global Fishing Watch analogy.

### What this changes for the paper

- The paper can state, with sources, that the IMF's own validation finds PortWatch weakest for small economies and small ports, and that tonnage levels are less reliable than changes. This supports the paper's choice to treat calls, not tonnage, as the usable Ro-Ro signal.
- The 96% match of port calls for Jamaica shows that call counts can be accurate for a small country once boundaries and port lists have been checked against the authorities. That check has not been done for Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan or Turkmenistan, and Kuryk is still missing from the port list. The paper should present its own comparison (section 5.3) as the first such check for the Caspian, not as a test of a method already validated there.
- Section 6 can add that no published validation exists for enclosed seas, rail ferries or Ro-Ro tonnage, with the search described above.
